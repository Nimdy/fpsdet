Every case says what made it.

A decision is a sentence a person reads. Under it, each case carries the same findings as data, what this client could have known, which checks could run at all, and digests of the code, the game settings, the baseline and the events that produced it. Score the same events with the same code, settings and baseline, and you get the same packet. Nothing here acts on its own. This does not end cheating.

Under the decision

What a case carries

The reasons a reviewer reads stay exactly as they were. Beside them, under evidence, the case keeps the same facts in a form a dashboard, a downstream model or another integrity system can read without parsing English.

observations

One record per finding.

Which check fired, the role it played in the decision, and the numbers it compared, with the matches it was measured on. The decision the observations imply is the case's decision, on every planted and real case.

knowledge

Known, unknowable, or unknown.

Every information check asks one question: could this client, with the official game, have seen or heard that enemy at that moment? Unchecked is not absent. A channel the server did not report makes the answer unknown, and the check abstains. Missing telemetry can only make fpsdet say less.

detector_eligibility

Which checks could run.

For every check: eligible, telemetry unavailable, disabled, or not applicable. A check your server gave no fields for is listed as unable to run. It is never a zero, and never a clean bill.

provenance

What produced it.

Digests of the detector code, the game profile as parsed, the human baseline it was compared with, the player's own events in the order the scorer read them, and the account history it checked. Two cases scored differently can be told apart without trusting whoever handed them over.

graph

What rests on what.

Typed nodes and edges: what supports the case and what is only context, what each finding was measured in, and which findings share a match, a challenge, a provider or a dependency. It records. It weighs nothing and decides nothing.

packet

One digest over all of it.

The packet binds the findings, the decision and everything above. Store it, and an edited case no longer matches. A digest proves content, not origin: whoever edits a case can recompute it. The seal still identifies the sentences a reviewer saw.

From examples/shot.jsonl, scored with --out. The player is insufficient_data, and the case still says exactly what produced that answer.

Active challenges

Probes planned with a secret.

A decoy becomes a challenge when the server plans it with a secret: at least 32 random bytes that never leave the game server. Each challenge is keyed to the game, the match, the player and its place in that player's match. The code is public, this recipe included. Without the secret, no one can compute when, where or how the next probe runs, even knowing every earlier one.

The plan holds ids, windows and commitments, and no realization. The game server derives where the body goes, keeps it while the challenge runs, and writes challenge_id and challenge_track_ms on the player's events. fpsdet binds a finding to that exact challenge. Scoring, reviewing and verifying a case never need the secret.

Version 2 of the challenge counts a moment only when the server's own verdict for that moment says the body was neither seen nor heard. One moment where it was seen or heard voids the challenge. So does a contradiction. A badly placed probe ends as an abstention, not as evidence.

fpsdet challenge keygen --out secret.hex
fpsdet challenge plan --profile game.json --match m-1 --player p-7 \
  --count 4 --secret-file secret.hex --out plan.json
fpsdet score events.ndjson --profile game.json --challenges plan.json
fpsdet challenge verify plan.json --secret-file secret.hex

The secret never appears in plan files, cases, packets, dashboards, these pages, AI prompts or test fixtures. A test draws a fresh one, plans and scores two players, and searches everything the run wrote.

Other systems

Their records ride along. They never make a review.

Carried as givenClient anti-cheat, platform attestation, a league's records: each one keeps its provider, its class and its confidence exactly as sent, in fpsdet.external/1 or through an adapter that is data, never code.

A watch at mostAn external record can move a clean case to watch. However many providers agree, it never makes a review. An account's ban history is context only.

Signed, not trueA provider can sign its records with Ed25519, checked against the operator's own registry of provider keys. Verified means the record came from that key. It does not mean the finding is right. No adapter or key for any commercial anti-cheat exists.

Where it stands

What has been tested, and how far it goes.

Every published statement carries one class, and no sentence mixes two. A planted fixture qualifies code. It is never a rate. A real dataset is one population with someone else's labels, and labels are not ground truth.

Class What ran What it showed What it is not
controlled fixture Every one of the 22 checks against a behaviour planted for it and an honest twin; the challenge and signature protocols. Each check trips on its plant and stays quiet on its twin. A detection rate. Plants are built to be caught.
benchmark v1 real data TF2 league matches: 10,149 server logs from logs.tf, 228 accounts RGL banned for cheating, never-banned players from the same lobbies, scored under 9 baseline draws fixed before any ran. Flagged for review or watch: a median 28.6% of the banned accounts it could compare, against 3.2% of the never-banned. 8.9× at the median, 7.7× to 10.4× across the draws. No never-banned player went to review in any draw. How well fpsdet detects cheating in general. Bans are labels, and most checks cannot be observed on match totals at all.
benchmark v1 real data 1,529 players in public CS2 matchmaking matches with hand-labelled cheaters (CS2CD), one match each. 18 sent to watch, 14 of them labelled cheaters. Nobody sent to review. Enough data per player to catch most cheaters. One match is too little.
live controlled pilot One challenge, run live in a Godot dedicated server with stock clients: planned with the secret, the probe moved by the server, its vision and audio checked every tick. All 8 declared scenarios came out as declared: a scripted follower reached review; an honest stand-in, a crosshair crossing, an exposed probe and a broken emitter did not. Every capture scored identically offline. A deployment or a detection rate. One engine, one map, scripted players.
consented human pilot Pre-registered: a target of 8 consenting adults, seven 4-minute sessions each, playing that arena honestly with probes running. Designed and instrumented. No person has played yet. A machine dry run did: a scripted stand-in holding a doorway's edge, still, while a probe stood behind it, counted 5,500 ms against a bar of 1,200 ms. Evidence about people. The stand-ins are scripts, and say so everywhere.
historical real data The first TF2 run: one baseline draw, chosen by a private key. 15.0×: the most favourable of its 6 draws, and higher than every draw of the benchmark. The result. It stays, verifiable, as a record.

Found before anyone played

Holding an angle can look like following.

The challenge bar counts time on the probe: 8 moments and 1,200 ms. It does not know why the aim was there. In the machine dry run, a stand-in held the left frame of a doorway. That session, the secret put a probe in the sealed room behind it. The probe replays a bot's recorded route, but only inside its small room, so it stays near one spot. The aim never turned. Over the challenge, the probe's median distance from the crosshair was 2.6°, inside the cone. It was held there in three spells of 1.1, 2.6 and 1.8 s, and fpsdet counted 5,500 ms. No visible bot explained it.

A person holding a doorway for a few seconds would do the same. The bar stays as it is while the human pilot measures how often people actually do this. The study stops at the first such case among people. The dry-run session is kept as a permanent test: any future challenge rule has to show it is no longer a review, and why.

Until then

  • A challenge review is a case to open, never a verdict. That was always true. It is now measured.
  • Read its episodes: aim that never turned, on a probe that barely moved, is the pattern above. Aim that moved with the probe as it moved is what the controlled follower does.
  • In the same dry run, that follower, the pilot's stand-in for a reader of hidden data, counted a median 13,258 ms per challenge.
  • Visible bots explained 55% of all honest-style overlap, and fpsdet left those moments out.

A run says MATCH, or exactly what moved and why.

What none of this proves

That a case is correct. Provenance says what produced it. A wrong visibility query, or a server that labels a visible enemy hidden, still manufactures the case.

How fpsdet does on your game. Each dataset here is one population. Your telemetry decides which checks can run at all.

That challenges are safe as a review on their own. Honest angle holding reached the bar in the dry run, and no person has played the pilot yet.

That fpsdet is better or worse than any other system. Nothing else was evaluated.

Who made a packet. A signature over the packet needs a server-held signing key, and is not built.
